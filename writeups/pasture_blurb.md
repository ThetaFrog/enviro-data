# **National Pastureland Percentages: A Cautionary Tale**

My interest in presenting data on pastureland percentages came from an infographic post on land cover/use in the UK. Amongst other things, it mentioned that half of the UK’s land was pastureland, which was a surprising statistic for me. Both in its presentation in this infographic and in my own understanding, pastureland could be used as a proxy for quantifying the environmental impact and importance of animal farming for different countries and regions. Therefore, I wanted to see how countries around the world (particularly Argentina, Australia and New Zealand, due to their animal product exports) compared to each other on this metric.

**The Data**  
The choropleth map I produced based on FAO data was a surprise. Some data was expected, like the high proportion of pastureland in the Falkland Islands and Australia. However, some of the countries with the highest percentage of pastureland (e.g Saudi Arabia) were unexpected. At nearly 80% pastureland, Saudi Arabia surpasses countries whose grazing industries are far more significant, locally and globally. However, this discrepancy is not the fault of Saudi Arabia or the FAO, but is rather the result of my own mistaken inference and expectation. In setting out to do my research, I made the (quite wrong) assumption that pastureland will in all cases be planted pastures made for intensive grazing. However, I neglected a number of factors in making this assumption.

**A lot of pastureland is natural**  
Pastureland doesn’t necessarily entail land planted or maintained by humans, or even land that is intensively grazed. In the case of Saudi Arabia, large parts of its land area are arid, making it difficult to use as cropland. Instead, it is used as low-density pastureland, which leads to its classification as such. We see similar reasons for the high percentages of pastureland seen in Mongolia, Kazakhstan and Uruguay: the land, due to its geography and flora, is better suited to grazing than it is to being used as cropland.

**Pastureland often doesn’t imply land taken away from other uses**  
From this, we gather that pastureland cannot really be used on its own as a proxy for the impact or importance of animal farming for specific countries: other factors are too important in pastureland classification. In many cases, the classification of land cover does not correspond to land modified from another type of land cover by human activities. The Eurasian steppe and the Pampas have historical and present uses as natural grazing land. Just as with forests and other land cover types, it is important for us to preserve these natural pasturelands and pragmatically calibrate our use of the land for human consumption with the necessity of preserving it for the future.

**How is pastureland classified then?**  
The FAO (where this data is sourced from) defines permanent pasture, which is the most statistically significant category to the total pastureland, as:

> “Land used permanently (five years or more) to grow herbaceous forage crops through cultivation or naturally (wild prairie or grazing land). [...] This class includes:
> * Grazing in wooded areas (agroforestry areas, for example);
> * Grazing in shrubby zones (heath, maquis, garigue);
> * Grassland in the plain or low mountain areas used for grazing;
> * Land crossed during transhumance where the animals spend a part of the year (approximately 100 days) without returning to the holding in the evening;
> * Mountain and subalpine meadows and similar;
> * and steppes and dry meadows used for pasture.”

This is of course a much broader definition than how I had (and how I expect others have) conceptualised pastureland before undertaking this research. This data therefore serves as a cautionary tale for myself and perhaps for others: that assumptions we make can mislead us and that definitions are crucial to properly understanding statistics quoted in isolation.
